# Short messages to senior colleagues

A message to a supervisor, a professor, a collaborator's lab head or a decision-maker goes out in the author's
name and must read as a person wrote it. Drafts built as documents, with headed points and lettered options,
were rejected as machine-made: "make this message more human, please."

## The form

1. **Short first-person paragraphs that tell a story**: what happens when, what comes after, the idea, the
   strategic point. One number where it matters, none elsewhere.
2. **Then at most three plain questions**, each answerable in a line.
3. **Nothing that looks like a document**: no headings, no bold labels, no "a) b)" options, no cost tables, no
   bullet lists of considerations.
4. **No furniture metaphors** ("where it lives", "sits at", "holds"). Say "is", "contains", "is given in".
5. **The author's own images**, followed by what they concretely mean.
6. **Kind and brief.** Ask plainly for the one thing needed, and carry the evidence; a soft or
   complaining note does not get it. The reviewer-facing form
   (quoted points, a table of concerns and revisions, one closing request) belongs to the `rebuttal` skill.
7. **Count the words before showing it.** One draft was cut from 505 to about 450 words.
8. **Dictated drafts carry speech-to-text errors.** Decode them from context and list each guess for the author.
9. **Give the message alone, ready to paste.** Sources, caveats, word counts and your guesses go after it,
   outside it, under a line that says they are not part of the message. When the author asks for the message
   again, give only the message. A draft answered with notes and sources around it drew "give me the slack
   message", and in another session a preview built as a document drew "don't make a complicated doc only the
   message".

Run the `humanizer` skill, where installed, on any sentence you had to compose, and keep every sentence the author
dictated as given.

## Example (invented)

Rejected shape:

> **Subject: Summer school, decisions needed**
>
> **1. Dates.** Option a) attend all two weeks; option b) attend the first week only.
> **2. Budget.** Estimated cost table below.
> **3. Where the poster lives.** ...

Accepted shape:

> Dear [Name],
>
> The summer school in July covers exactly the methods my second chapter needs, and I would like to go with a
> poster of the first chapter. The point is to get feedback on the analysis before I write it up.
>
> I would attend the first week, which holds the two courses I need, and be back for the lab retreat. Travel and
> fees come to about 900 euros, which fits the travel budget we set in January.
>
> Three questions: are you happy with one week instead of two? Can the fees come from the travel budget? And would
> you look at the poster draft before I submit it?
>
> Best,
> [Name]

About 120 words, three questions, one number, no headings.
