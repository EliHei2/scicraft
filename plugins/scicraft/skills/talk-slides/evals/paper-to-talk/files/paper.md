# A sweat patch that flags sepsis hours before the bedside score

(Fictional manuscript, written as a test fixture. None of these results are real.)

## Abstract

Sepsis kills because it is recognized late. Bedside scores rely on vital signs that change only after organ
function has begun to fail. We present SweatSense, a flexible skin patch that measures lactate, cortisol and
interleukin-6 in sweat every five minutes. In a prospective study of 412 adults admitted to two intensive care
units, the patch raised an alert a median of 6.2 hours before the bedside score crossed its threshold. At the
alert, the patch identified 88 of 97 patients who went on to develop sepsis, with 31 false alerts among 315
patients who did not. The patch needs no blood draw and no trained operator. Continuous sweat chemistry gives
clinicians a window in which sepsis can still be prevented.

## Introduction

Every hour of delay in treating sepsis raises mortality. Yet the tools used to recognize it were designed for a
different question. Bedside scores summarize heart rate, blood pressure, breathing and temperature, and these
change only after the infection has started to damage organs. Blood tests are informative but they are drawn a
few times a day, and each draw needs a nurse. What should be measured continuously, in every patient, without a
needle? Sweat carries small molecules and signaling proteins that follow their blood levels within minutes.
Until now, sweat sensors measured one molecule at a time and drifted within hours. We asked whether three
markers measured together, on one patch that corrects its own drift, could warn of sepsis earlier than the score
that wards use today.

## Results

The patch stayed attached and within calibration for a median of 71 hours. Its lactate reading followed arterial
blood lactate with a correlation of 0.91 across 3,280 paired samples. The alert combined the three markers and
was fixed before the study began. Of 412 patients, 97 developed sepsis. The patch alerted before onset in 88 of
them, a median of 6.2 hours (interquartile range 3.9 to 9.4) before the bedside score. Among the 315 patients
without sepsis, 31 received an alert. Nurses rated the patch as easier to manage than a pulse oximeter in 79% of
shifts. The alert performed the same in both hospitals and in patients over 75 years.

## Discussion

Continuous sweat chemistry gives clinicians a window in which sepsis can still be prevented. The patch does not
replace the bedside score; it tells the team which patients to look at first. The next step is a randomized
trial in which an alert triggers an early review by the sepsis team. The patch is being prepared for use on
general wards, where patients are checked only every few hours and where most deaths from sepsis begin.
